METHOD

Twenty questions,
for pipes.

A main has cracked somewhere under the district. The utility knows water is leaving — more goes in than comes out of taps — but not where. Thirty-five junctions could be responsible.

Checking one means two people, a truck and a pressure gauge: about an hour. Opening the wrong street costs roughly twenty thousand dollars.

Rivus simulates a leak at every junction using the EPA's hydraulic engine — thirty-six runs in a fifth of a second — and each possible break leaves a different fingerprint of pressure drops.

Then it does the part nobody else does: it names the single hydrant whose reading will eliminate the most suspects, and updates the moment the crew reports back.

01

Simulate every suspect

One hydraulic run per candidate junction. A leak anywhere moves pressure everywhere, so one run scores every possible gauge at once.

02

Choose the next reading

Every candidate hydrant is scored by expected information gain, weighed against the crew-hours it costs to get there.

03

Say what cannot be known

Junctions on the same run of pipe are hydraulically identical. Rivus names those pairs instead of picking a confident winner.

04

Screen the shutdown

Every valve plan is checked twice — graph reachability and a pressure-dependent run — before anyone closes anything.

LIMITS

What we don't know,
on the record.

The exact pipe is found about 10–20% of the time. Rivus reports a credible set. Top-1 is not a call it makes.

It needs a network with structure. On a small, hydraulically degenerate district it is no better than guessing, and it says so.

Shutdown screening is modelled service impact, not a safety certification. An operator decides.

The sample networks are EPA references, not a utility's own calibrated model. The engine reads standard EPANET files, so a real one drops straight in.
